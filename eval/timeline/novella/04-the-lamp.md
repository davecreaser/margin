# Chapter 3

Two days after the ferry left, the wind dropped for an hour at low water, and Maren went down to the north shore to see what the sea had brought.

The sea had brought a great deal. Weed in heaps as high as her waist, a drowned sheep, a fish-box from Ardmore, a boot. Among the rocks below the causeway she found the nameboard of a ship, split down the middle, with the gilt still bright on the letters RINNA.

She sat down on a wet rock with the board across her knees.

In the winter of 1871 her mother had walked out onto the causeway at dusk to bring in a ewe that had strayed, and the tide had come round behind her faster than anyone on Carrick could remember a tide coming. They found her shawl on the second rock and nothing else. Maren had been nine. She remembered that her father had lit the lamp that night as usual, at the usual hour, because the Board required it, and that she had hated him for it for a year and then understood.

She carried the nameboard up to the light and leaned it against the oil store, letters to the wall, so that her father would not see it.

That night the gale came back worse than before, and the glass in the lantern room hummed like a struck bowl. Maren kept the lamp burning through the whole of the night alone, and did not call her father for his watch. At every flash she looked north towards the Skerries, where there was nothing to see, and counted out loud.
