# Chapter 1

The gales came to Carrick Island in the autumn of 1882, and on the first morning of the gales the ferry left the island at nine, an hour late, with Tobias Venn standing in the stern and not once looking back.

His sister Maren watched it from the lamp gallery. She had argued with him until midnight and lost, as she lost most arguments with Tobias, by being right too early. He meant to sign on a coal brig at Port Ellery and see something of the world that was not grey water and grey rock. She meant him to stay and help with the light, because their father's hands shook now and the Board would not keep a keeper whose hands shook.

All that morning she worked as if the work could be finished. She polished the brass of the lens until she could see her own cross face in it twelve times over. She trimmed the wicks and carried oil up the hundred and four steps, two cans at a time. Her father sat in the kitchen with the almanac open at the tide tables and did not read it.

At noon the wind backed into the south-west and the sea came up white along the causeway. The gulls gave up and sat on the roof of the store, all facing the same way, like a congregation.

At dusk Maren lit the lamp. It caught at the third match, and the light went round, and she stood beside it in the warm smell of paraffin and counted the flashes out loud, the way her mother had taught her, so that she would not think about the ferry.

Her father came up at ten to take the first watch. He looked at the barometer and tapped it, and the needle fell a little further.

"He'll be in Port Ellery by now," he said.

"He'll be in a tavern in Port Ellery by now," said Maren, and went down to bed, and lay awake listening to the shutters.
