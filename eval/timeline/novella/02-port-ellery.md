# Chapter 2

Tobias reached Port Ellery a little before noon, soaked to the knees, with eleven shillings in his pocket and his father's sea-chest on his shoulder. The ferryman had put him ashore at the wrong steps and charged him twice for the privilege.

The town smelled of coal and fish and wet horses. He walked the length of the quay twice before he found the Corinna, a squat collier brig lying low in the water with her hatches already battened. Her master, a Captain Rudd, looked him over as a man looks at a horse he does not mean to buy.

"Island boy," said Rudd. "Can you climb?"

"I've climbed a lighthouse every day of my life."

"A lighthouse holds still."

That same evening, while his sister was lighting the lamp seventy miles away, Tobias signed on the Corinna as ordinary seaman, at two pounds ten a month and his keep. He wrote his name in the book in his best schoolroom hand, and the mate, reading it upside down, laughed at the loops on the capital T.

He was given a hammock in the fo'c'sle between a Welshman who did not speak and a boy from Hull who did not stop. The boy told him the Corinna was a lucky ship. The Welshman turned over in his hammock and spat, which Tobias took to be a second opinion.

He did not sleep. The brig worked against her moorings all night, and every time the hull groaned he heard his sister's voice telling him that he would not last a week. He resolved, somewhere about two in the morning, to last a year out of spite.
